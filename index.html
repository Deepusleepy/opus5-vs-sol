What counted as a win?

A blind judge compared both submissions against the full task—not just their self-written tests—and picked the stronger result. There was no weighted formula.

Works correctlyIncluding failure paths.
Follows the contractExact API and behavior.
Handles edge casesConcurrency, cancellation, bad input.
Proves the behaviorUseful adversarial tests.
Ships the whole taskWithout needless machinery.

Was Opus code actually better?

Usually more complete and safer. Not always cleaner.

Opus was stronger at

  • Matching subtle parts of the spec
  • Handling failure paths and bad inputs
  • Writing adversarial, deterministic tests
  • Finding and grading defects

Sol was stronger at

  • Finishing faster on 11 of 14 comparisons
  • Keeping implementations smaller
  • Avoiding unnecessary files and layers
  • Planning the cleaner migration sequence

Opus earned its wins when the extra work caught a real failure. Sol often had the cleaner core implementation, but its thinner tests missed more of the contract.

Three examples

Why a green suite did not settle the comparison.

T2 · Sol

Test matched its implementation

The cancellation handoff worked only for the exact error shape returned by its own fake loader. Opus handled a realistic loader error.

T4 · Sol

Injected time still used real time

High effort fixed the refill math, but the fake clock still drove real-time polling. Opus could wake blocked tests through the clock itself.

T7 · Sol

A guard rejected a valid call

After a render-phase error, the guard stayed active for a microtask and rejected the next legitimate update. High effort kept the behavior.

Method and limits

What I ran and what it cannot prove.

Setup

Nine tasks received one medium-effort output from each model. Five coding tasks were rerun at high effort. Fable orchestrated and adjudicated; Opus- and Sol-family judges reviewed anonymous A/B trees.

Public artifacts include prompts, outputs, timings, mappings, and judge reports.

Limits

  • One run per model, task, and effort.
  • Systems-heavy tasks; little UI or product work.
  • Two judge families, not four independent ones.
  • T2, T3, and T8 were re-judged without a high-effort rerun.
  • T9 used an answer key and sits outside the blind tally.
Run on 28 July 2026. Small firsthand eval, not a universal leaderboard.
